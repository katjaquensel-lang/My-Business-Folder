# Craft Fundamentals

> The mechanics underneath every format. format-specs.md says WHICH move to make in WHICH slot.
> This file teaches HOW to make each move land. Every command reads this before drafting.
> These are taught as patterns, not scripts. Apply them in the member's voice, never as templates.

---

## 1. Write to one person, in her words

The single highest-leverage move. Copy written for "everyone" moves no one.

How to do it well:
- Pull the exact phrasing your one person uses from her own mouth: her DMs, her reviews, the
  questions she asks, the way she describes the problem to a friend. Use HER nouns, not industry nouns.
- Test: could this line have been written about a different person in a different business? If yes,
  it is too generic. Add the detail only she would recognise.
- The goal line is "she feels read," not "she is impressed." Impressed is about you. Read is about her.

The move that makes her feel read: name the thought she has had but never said out loud. Not the
feeling (everyone names the feeling), the specific 3am thought in her exact words.
- Weak shape: a label for the feeling. "It can feel discouraging when growth is slow."
- Strong shape: the unspoken thought, in her words. "[The exact question she Googles at 11pm.]"

## 2. Benefits before features (the "so what" ladder)

People do not buy the thing. They buy who they get to become because of the thing.

How to do it well:
- For every feature, climb the ladder: feature -> what it does -> what that means for her ->
  who she becomes. Lead with the top of the ladder, support with the bottom.
- Pattern: "[Feature]" becomes "So you can [outcome]" becomes "Which means [identity/life change]."
- Put a real number on the benefit wherever one exists. "Saves time" is weak. "[X] hours back a week"
  is a benefit she can feel. (Use a real number or ask the member for one. Never invent it.)
- Rule of thumb: she has to WANT the outcome before she cares HOW it works. Earn the right to list
  features by selling the after first.

## 3. Name the pain, then sell the pleasure (the 20/80 rule)

A little "I know exactly where you are," then most of your energy on "here is where you could be."

How to do it well:
- Roughly a fifth of the energy on pain, the rest on vision. Pain gets her attention; pleasure is
  what she buys. Too much pain and the copy feels like wallowing or manipulation.
- Make the pain specific and physical (a scene, a time of day, a sensation), not a diagnosis.
  "You are overwhelmed" is a diagnosis. "[The specific moment the overwhelm shows up]" is a scene.
- Make the pleasure specific and daily, the exact opposite of the pain scene. If the pain was the
  Sunday-night dread, the pleasure is the Sunday night that feels different. Mirror the two.
- Never sell the pain. Sell the relief and the after. Name the friction briefly, then live in the vision.

## 4. The reframe (the most important and the hardest move)

Take what she blames for being stuck, and show her the real, hidden cause she had not seen.

Why it works: it lifts the blame off her, so she stops being defensive, and it makes you the one
person who finally explained it. A good reframe is the spine of a great sales page and the turn in
a great newsletter.

How to FIND a reframe when the member cannot articulate one (interview them with these):
- "What does your person blame herself for? What does she think is wrong with HER?"
- "What is actually causing that, that is NOT her fault? The thing she cannot see from inside it?"
- "What do you know, from experience, that she does not know yet?"
- The reframe lives in the gap between what she blames (usually herself, or effort) and the real
  cause (usually a system, a sequence, a missing piece she was never taught).

How to WRITE it without the cliche:
- The shape is "the problem was never [what she blames], it is [the hidden cause]."
- But NEVER write it in the "it's not X, it's Y" construction. That sentence shape is banned and it
  reads as AI. Find the member's own way to say it: a story, a confession, a plain statement.
- Strong reframe test: does it make her exhale (relief) and then lean in (curiosity)? If it only
  informs, it is not a reframe, it is a fact.

## 5. Prove it honestly (specificity + admission)

Specifics feel like fact. Admitting a flaw makes everything else believable.

How to do it well:
- Pair every claim with a real, specific number AND a real messy detail or admission. Polish alone
  reads as a brochure. "[Specific result]" plus "[the part that went wrong first]" reads as true.
- Specific beats big. "[A precise, odd number]" is more believable than a round impressive one.
- The honest admission is a persuasion tool, not a weakness. The flaw you admit buys belief for the
  claim you make next. Use it deliberately, right before the strongest claim.
- Never invent proof. If the member has no number or result yet, the honest version is the founder's
  own story and conviction. Build the proof section from what is real, even if it is small.

---

## 6. One idea, one action

Especially anything that sells. A piece trying to do two jobs does neither.

How to do it well:
- Before writing, name the ONE thing the reader should think, feel, and DO. Everything serves that.
- If a second good idea shows up, it is the next piece, not this one. Cut it and save it.
- One primary call to action per email or page. Extra choices reduce action. Repeat the one CTA,
  do not add new ones.

---

## 7. The curiosity gap (for every opener: subject lines, hooks, first lines)

You earn the next line, every line. The first job of any sentence is to make the next one get read.

How to do it well:
- Open a gap she needs closed: a question she cannot help answering, a number that promises a story,
  a confession a brand normally would not make, a statement that contradicts what she expects.
- The gap must be real. If the payoff does not deliver on the curiosity, you teach her to stop opening.
  Clickbait works once.
- Specific out-curiosities vague every time. A precise, slightly odd detail pulls harder than a big
  abstract promise.
